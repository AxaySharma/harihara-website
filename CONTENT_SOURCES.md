## 1. Elemental Bromine Characteristics (#bromine)

### Chemical & Physical Properties
- **Statement**: "Symbol: Br | Atomic number: 35 | At room temperature: Liquid | Family: Halogens"
- **Statement**: "Bromine is a dense, reddish-brown liquid with a sharp smell. Along with mercury, it is one of only two elements that are liquid at normal room temperature."
- **Fact Verification**:
  - Bromine has atomic number 35 and symbol Br; it belongs to Group 17 (the halogens).
  - Along with mercury (Hg), bromine is one of only two chemical elements in the periodic table that exist in liquid state at standard room temperature and pressure ($25^\circ\text{C}$, $1\text{ atm}$).
  - Density is approx. $3.102\text{ g/cm}^3$, appearing as a fuming reddish-brown liquid.
- **Sources**:

## 2. Occurrence in Bittern & Sea Salt Residues (#bromine, #process)

### Occurrence & Concentration in Bittern
- **Statement**: "Bromine occurs as bromide, a compound dissolved in seawater, salt lakes and natural brines. When seawater is evaporated to make salt, the bromide stays behind and becomes concentrated in the leftover brine, called bittern."
- **Statement**: "After salt crystallises out of evaporating seawater, a mineral-rich liquid remains."
- **Fact Verification**:
  - Seawater contains approximately 65 to 68 mg/L of bromide ions ($\text{Br}^-$).
  - During solar salt production, sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) crystallises out first between $25^\circ$ and $29^\circ\text{ Be}'$.
  - The leftover residual liquor (density $>29^\circ\text{ Be}'$), known technically and commercially as *bittern*, contains concentrated soluble minerals including magnesium chloride, potassium chloride, and high concentrations of bromide ions ($2.0\text{ to }4.5\text{ g/L}$).
- **Sources**:
  - CSIR - Central Salt and Marine Chemicals Research Institute (CSIR-CSMCRI), Bhavnagar, Gujarat: https://www.csmcri.res.in
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica - Bittern (chemical solution): https://www.britannica.com/science/bittern-chemical-solution
  - USGS Minerals - Bromine: https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-bromine.pdf

## 3. History of Discovery & Etymology (#bromine)

### Discovery by Antoine-Jérôme Balard (1826)
- **Statement**: "Bromine was first isolated in the 1820s by the French chemist Antoine-Jérôme Balard, who found it in the residue of salt-marsh brine near Montpellier."
- **Statement**: "The name comes from the Greek word bromos, meaning stench, because of its strong smell."
- **Fact Verification**:
  - Antoine-Jérôme Balard isolated elemental bromine in 1826 at Montpellier, France, while investigating residual mother liquors ("bittern") from sea salt evaporation pans along the Mediterranean coast.
  - The Académie des Sciences officially adopted the name *brome* (bromine), derived from the Ancient Greek $\beta\rho\tilde{\omega}\mu\text{o}\varsigma$ (*brômos*), meaning "stench" or "bad smell", reflecting its sharp, suffocating vapors.
  - *(Note on Carl Löwig: German chemist Carl Löwig also independently isolated bromine in 1825 from spring water in Bad Kreuznach, but Balard published his findings first in 1826. Framing as "first isolated in the 1820s by the French chemist Antoine-Jérôme Balard" is accurate and accepted worldwide).*
- **Sources**:
  - Encyclopaedia Britannica - Antoine-Jérôme Balard: https://www.britannica.com/biography/Antoine-Jerome-Balard
  - Royal Society of Chemistry - History of Bromine: https://www.rsc.org/periodic-table/element/35/bromine

## 4. Safety & Handling (#bromine)

### Toxic & Corrosive Nature
- **Statement**: "Bromine is toxic and corrosive. This is why its production and handling are regulated, and why any plant that works with it must meet strict safety and environmental requirements."
- **Fact Verification**:
  - Liquid bromine produces toxic, heavy red vapors that severely irritate and damage the eyes, skin, and respiratory tract. It is a powerful oxidising agent classified as corrosive (Class 8) and toxic (Class 6.1) under UN hazardous materials transport regulations and national factory safety statutes.
- **Sources**:
  - International Chemical Safety Cards (ICSC 0107 - Bromine, WHO/ILO/UNEP): https://www.ilo.org/dyn/icsc/showcard.display?p_card_id=0107
  - US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards - Bromine: https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/npgd0064.html

## 5. Industrial Applications (#industries, #products)

### 01 Pharmaceutical
- **Statement**: "Bromine compounds serve as reagents and building blocks in making some medicines."
- **Fact Verification**: Bromine is widely used in organic synthesis as a halogenating and brominating reagent to introduce bromine into pharmaceutical intermediates, sedative bromide salts, APIs, and complex active therapeutic molecules.
- **Source**: Royal Society of Chemistry / American Chemical Society: https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.joc.1c01500

### 02 Agrochemical
- **Statement**: "Some crop-protection chemicals are made using bromine-based intermediates."
- **Fact Verification**: Brominated organic intermediates are key synthetic precursors for fungicides, insecticides, and herbicides in modern crop protection.
- **Source**: USGS Bromine Mineral Commodity Summaries: https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2024/mcs2024-bromine.pdf

### 03 Flame Retardants
- **Statement**: "Brominated compounds are added to certain plastics, textiles and electronic parts to slow the spread of fire."
- **Fact Verification**: Brominated flame retardants (BFRs) represent one of the largest industrial applications of bromine, functioning by releasing bromine radicals at combustion temperatures to terminate chemical radical chain reactions in polymer matrices, printed circuit boards, and textile fibers.
- **Source**: European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) / Britannica: https://www.britannica.com/technology/flame-retardant

### 04 Water Treatment
- **Statement**: "Bromine-based chemicals are used to disinfect some cooling-water systems and swimming pools."
- **Fact Verification**: Bromine chemistry (hypobromous acid, BCDMH) is widely used in industrial recirculating cooling towers, process water, and recreational pools, particularly effective in warmer or higher-pH waters where chlorine efficiency diminishes.
- **Source**: US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Water Treatment Guidance: https://www.epa.gov

### 05 Oil-Field Chemicals
- **Statement**: "Dense bromide brines are used in drilling and completing oil and gas wells."
- **Fact Verification**: High-density clear brine fluids (calcium bromide $\text{CaBr}_2$, sodium bromide $\text{NaBr}$, and zinc bromide $\text{ZnBr}_2$) are standard completion and workover fluids in petroleum engineering, providing hydraulic head pressure without solids to prevent formation damage.
- **Source**: Society of Petroleum Engineers (SPE) PetroWiki - Completion fluids: https://petrowiki.spe.org/Completion_fluids

## 6. Geographic & Regional Context (#location)

### Kachchh District & Gujarat Salt Industry
- **Statement**: "Kachchh, in western Gujarat, is India's largest district by area. Salt is produced in and around the district, which makes it a natural home for a plant that works with bittern."
- **Fact Verification**:
  - Kachchh (Kutch) covers approximately $45,674\text{ km}^2$, making it officially the largest district in the Republic of India by geographical area according to Census of India administrative records and Government of Gujarat records.
  - The state of Gujarat accounts for over 75% to 80% of India's total solar marine salt production (under the purview of the Salt Commissioner's Organisation, Ministry of Commerce and Industry, Government of India), with major salt work pans clustered in coastal Kachchh and the Rann.
- **Sources**:
  - District Census Handbook - Kachchh, Directorate of Census Operations, Gujarat / Census of India: https://censusindia.gov.in
  - Official Portal of District Administration, Kachchh: https://kachchh.nic.in
  - Salt Commissioner's Organisation (SCO), Government of India: https://salt.gov.in
